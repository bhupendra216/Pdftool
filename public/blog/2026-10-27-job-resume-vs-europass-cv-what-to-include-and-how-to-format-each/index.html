Job Resume vs Europass CV: What to Include and How to Format Each

October 27, 2026 By PDFKira Team 7 min read

If you are applying for jobs in more than one country, you will notice that “resume” and “CV” don’t mean the same thing everywhere. A US recruiter expects a short, results-focused page. An employer in Germany, Italy or Spain may expect a longer CV with personal details, language levels and sometimes a photo. This guide explains the differences and how to build each version without wasting time.

The short version

A job resume is one page for most early-career applicants. It shows what you did and what changed because of it. A Europass-style CV is usually up to two pages. It is more structured and includes personal details and language skills. The Europass format is a European standard. The version in our Resume & CV Builder follows that structure but is not an official Europass template.

What goes in a job resume

Keep it to what helps a recruiter decide in about thirty seconds:

Header: name, email, phone, city, and one or two links such as LinkedIn or a portfolio.

Summary (optional): two lines about who you are and what you do. Skip it if it only repeats the rest of the page.

Experience: latest first. For each role, give the title, organization, dates and two to four bullets that show results. “Reduced handoff delays by creating a shared checklist” says more than “responsible for handoffs”.

Education: degree, school and years. Early-career applicants can put it above Experience.

Skills: group them (for example tools, methods, languages) and list only what you can use.

In the US, UK and Canada, leave out your photo, date of birth, marital status and full home address. Employers there don’t expect them, and some prefer not to see them.

What goes in a Europass-style CV

A Europass-style CV keeps the same core sections and adds some that European employers expect:

Personal details: address, nationality and date of birth are common in many countries, though not required everywhere. Include them only when the country or employer expects them.

Photo: common in parts of Europe, unusual elsewhere. Use a plain, recent headshot, and treat it as optional.

Summary first: a short profile at the top.

Languages: mother tongue, then each other language rated on the CEFR scale (A1 to C2) for listening, reading, spoken interaction, spoken production and writing. Add a certificate such as DELE or DELF if you have one.

Digital skills: rate your skills as Basic, Independent or Proficient, with a few concrete examples.

Extras: driving licence category, memberships, conferences and grants, when they are relevant.

Layout rules that work for both

Parsers and recruiters both read a simple layout best:

Use one column. Two-column layouts and tables can scramble the reading order.

Use standard headings such as Experience, Education and Skills.

Keep dates in one format throughout, for example 2024–Present.

Avoid text inside images or text boxes, because a parser may skip it.

Export a PDF with real text. If you can’t select and copy the text, neither can a parser.

How to check your PDF

Open the PDF and try to select a line of text. Press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on Mac) and search for your name. Copy everything into a plain text file and look at the order. Your header should come first, with sections following in order.

If words are missing, joined together or out of order, simplify the layout.
